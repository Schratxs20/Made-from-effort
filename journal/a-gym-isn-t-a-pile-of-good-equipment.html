A Gym Isn't a Pile of Good Equipment

By Scott Schratwieser · Published October 5, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026

Most of the "bad gym" calls I get don't start with bad equipment. They start with good equipment that was bought one piece at a time.

A rack because it looked right in a photo. A cable machine because a friend has one. A sled, because why not — there's a corner for it. Taken individually, every piece is a reasonable choice. Taken together, they add up to a room nobody can actually move through.

A residential gym design plan has to account for the space around each piece before anything is ordered.

01 / The Math Nobody Does Up Front

Equipment doesn't just take up the floor space it sits on. It takes up the space around it — room to rack a bar overhead, step back from a squat, swing a kettlebell without clipping a corner. Buy each piece on its own merits and that clearance math never happens; it just gets discovered later, one bruised knuckle at a time.

A room full of good equipment and no clearance isn't a gym. It's storage you can technically stand in.

I ran into this directly in a narrow residential gym where the full program still had to fit: strength, cardio, recovery, all of it. Piece by piece, that room fails before the second purchase. The only way it works is treating the whole footprint as one system before a single item gets ordered: what has to be fixed to a wall, what can share a footprint at different points in a session, where a body actually needs to stand to use each piece safely.

The same problem can build over time. This club gym was outfitted more than 20 years ago and now needs a refresh. Its plan assigns space to a long list of stations, while the room shows how little open floor remains between them. The point is not to replace older machines one-for-one. It is to replan the room around how people train now.

An older equipment plan with stations distributed across the footprint. A refresh should plan circulation as deliberately as equipment.

A crowded fitness room with treadmills and strength equipment competing for floor area. A refresh begins by deciding which equipment serves the current training program.

02 / What "System" Actually Means

It's not a style choice. It means every piece earns its spot based on what it does for the room as a whole, not how it looks on its own.

The anchor piece gets placed first, and everything else gets arranged around the clearance it needs — not the other way around.

Shared space needs an operating plan. Show where equipment sits during use and where it goes afterward. Stored equipment must not obstruct required routes or access; active stations must retain manufacturer-required clearances. Where ADA provisions apply, check the accessible route and the space for transfer or seated use. Permitted overlap of some accessibility spaces does not authorize an obstruction in a manufacturer's safety zone. ADA Sections 206.2.13 and 236; Section 1004.

The walking path gets decided before the equipment does. If you can't move from one station to the next without turning sideways, the room was planned backwards — equipment first, person second.

For coached sessions, show the trainer's sightline and access for assistance as well as the client's movement. Those are practical supervision considerations in the NSCA Professional Standards and Guidelines. A circulation line on a plan is not the whole review.

ADA applicability depends on the facility's use. A solely private home is generally outside its coverage, while inclusive access can still be part of the brief. U.S. Access Board scope guidance.

03 / Why This Gets Skipped

Because "equipment package" is an easy thing to sell and an easy thing to buy. A system is slower — it means figuring out how someone actually moves through a session before any of it gets ordered, which isn't as fun as picking finishes.

But it's the difference between a room that gets used daily and one that becomes an expensive place to store things you meant to use.

If you're planning a build, start with how a body moves through the space — not with a wish list of equipment. The equipment is supposed to serve that movement. The minute it stops serving it, you don't have a gym. You have a very well-furnished storage room.
