The Program Isn't the Product
September 17, 2026 · Mindset

Every new client assumes the program is the thing. I write it, they follow it, results happen. That's the transaction as they see it.

It's not what actually happens.

01 / What You're Not Seeing

By the time a client sees their program, I've already thrown out ten versions of it that weren't right for them. Not because I got it wrong ten times — because getting to the right one requires generating way more options than you'll ever use, then cutting almost all of them.

Most people think the skill is designing the workout. The actual skill is the cutting. Anyone can write a program that looks reasonable on paper. Writing the one that survives contact with this specific person's schedule, injury history, and honest tolerance for discomfort means rejecting far more than you keep.

If I only ever generated one option and ran with it, I'd be right by luck, not by design.

02 / The Threshold Nobody Sees You Apply

Before anything goes to a client, it has to pass a test that has nothing to do with whether the exercises are "good." The test is whether it actually fits their life — not their ideal life, their real one. A program that would work perfectly for someone with two hours and no kids gets rejected for someone with forty minutes and a toddler, even if the first version is objectively the better program in a vacuum.

That threshold kills more programs than any technical flaw does. A plan that's 90% as effective but 100% sustainable beats a plan that's 100% effective and 60% sustainable, every time. I'd rather reject the better program than hand someone something they can't actually keep doing.

The program that gets used beats the program that gets admired.

03 / Why the Same Program Gets Different Results

I've handed two clients nearly identical programs and watched one stick with it for a year and the other quit in three weeks. The exercises weren't the variable. How the plan was framed for each of them was.

One client needed to hear it as "this is the floor — anything above this is a bonus," because that took the pressure off enough for her to actually start. Another needed the opposite: a hard number to hit, because ambiguity was what let him talk himself out of showing up. Same underlying structure, opposite packaging, because the packaging is what determines whether a person actually buys in — and buy-in is what determines whether the program ever gets tested against reality at all.

What this means if you're the one building the plan, for yourself or someone else:

  1. Generate more than you need, then cut hard. The first version you write is rarely the one that should survive. If nothing gets rejected, you probably didn't generate enough options to begin with.
  2. "Fits their life" beats "technically superior" almost every time. A program measured only against an ideal week will lose to one measured against an honest one.
  3. How it's framed isn't decoration — it's part of whether it works. The same structure can succeed or fail depending on whether the person buys into it, and that's a real design variable, not an afterthought.
  4. Small, consistent effort compounds in ways that don't show up right away. The results from good filtering and good framing aren't visible in week one. They're visible in month six, when the other version already got abandoned.

None of this is about working harder on the workout itself. It's about doing the harder, less visible work before the client ever sees it — and building the plan for the person you actually have, not the person a perfect program would assume.

FAQ

Why would a "worse" program ever beat a better one?

Because a program only produces results if someone actually does it. A slightly less optimal plan that gets followed consistently outperforms a superior plan that gets abandoned in week three, every time.

How do you know which way to frame a plan for a given client?

Usually it comes out in how they talk about past attempts — whether they describe past failures as "I didn't hit the target" (respond well to a hard number) or "it felt like too much" (respond better to a floor, not a ceiling).

Does this mean the actual exercises don't matter?

They matter — they're just not the part most people assume is the hard part. The exercise selection is often the easiest piece to get right. Getting a client to actually do them, consistently, is the real work.
