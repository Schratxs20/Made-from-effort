Planning a Private Gym Before the Floor Plan Is Finished

By Scott Schratwieser · Published October 30, 2026

How much room do we need for the gym?

I want the first gym plan to show a training session. Start by walking through one. Where does the warm-up happen? Where does the bench move? Can a second person train while the first is using the cable station?

The answers give the architect something to draw. A list of equipment gives only part of that information.

The machine is only one measurement

Consider a treadmill. The Life Fitness T3 owner's manual, revision B-31, lists the machine at 79.5 inches long and 34.5 inches wide. It also calls for a clear area behind it measuring 6.5 feet by 3 feet. That rear area must be free of obstructions, including furniture and other equipment. Life Fitness T3 owner's manual, pages 5 and 22.

Draw only the machine, and nearly another machine-length of required space is missing from the plan.

That changes the conversation about the bench behind it, the storage along the wall, and where someone else can exercise. The empty area in the drawing has a purpose.

This is one manufacturer's published example, not a universal treadmill layout. The selected model's current instructions, access needs, and the rest of the room still have to be checked. A rectangle that accommodates this example does not establish that the entire gym works.

Plan the session between the equipment

As a planning example, imagine someone warming up on a mat, using dumbbells and a bench, then moving to the treadmill.

Place the mat on the plan. Move the bench into its working position. Open the storage. Add a trainer standing where they can see the movement.

Now look for conflicts. Does the open drawer occupy the space needed to pick up a weight? Does the bench have to cross the entrance? Is the warm-up area also the treadmill's required clear area?

If two people will train together, draw both activities at the same time. A room may support every exercise individually and still fail the session the owners intend to share.

The NSCA's professional standards call for a clear view of the people being coached and quick access when assistance is needed. They also require equipment setup to follow manufacturer instructions. That gives the trainer's position a practical place on the drawing: can they see, communicate, and reach the person they are coaching? NSCA Professional Standards and Guidelines, supervision and Standard 4.1.

My view is that these ordinary transitions deserve a place in the first layout. They explain how the room will be used more clearly than a fully populated equipment plan.

Check the height while the room can change

Equipment height alone does not describe the space a moving person needs.

Life Fitness's Arc Trainer guidance considers the user's height together with the raised pedal position when assessing overhead clearance. That is a useful reminder to look above the equipment outline. Confirm the requirements for the actual model and users rather than transferring a general allowance from another machine. Life Fitness Arc Trainer clearance guidance.

A ceiling section can reveal what the floor plan cannot: a beam above an overhead movement, a sloping ceiling over a cardio station, or a light fitting in the space someone needs to use.

Bring those questions to the architect before the equipment positions are treated as settled.

Who else needs to use the room?

Include the other people who may use the room: a partner, a guest, a trainer, or someone using a mobility aid. Consider how each person enters, approaches the equipment, and uses it. A transfer or seated exercise may need space that an equipment outline does not show.

For facilities within the applicable ADA scope, the 2010 Standards require access to at least one of each equipment type, with appropriately positioned clear floor space. That space is an accessibility provision, not a replacement for manufacturer safety clearances. ADA Standards, Sections 206.2.13 and 236 and Section 1004.

A private home used only as a residence is generally outside ADA coverage. Inclusive planning can still be part of the owner's brief. Business use, hospitality, and other project conditions need an applicability review with the architect, including relevant state and local requirements. U.S. Access Board: Using the ADA Standards.

Decide what the building needs to know

The first gym brief does not need every finish and accessory selected. It needs to identify the decisions that affect the building.

Question Information to bring to the project team
Who trains together? Activities that must work at the same time, including coaching space.
What moves during a session? Bench positions, cable movements, open storage, and manufacturer clearances.
What happens above and below the equipment? User movement and height, equipment loads, and intended training activities for the responsible technical team to assess.
How does equipment reach the room? The delivery and assembly route, including doors, turns, and stairs.
What remains undecided? Each open choice, who will resolve it, and which drawing or order depends on it.

Floor loading, acoustics, ventilation, power, and installation requirements need answers from the appropriate architect, engineer, builder, or equipment specialist. The brief makes the questions visible; it does not replace their work.

At the next plan review, ask three questions: Where do I begin? What moves during the session? What must stay clear while someone else is training?

Mark the answers on the drawing. Any conflict is a decision to resolve while the room can still change.

Equipment reference: The T3 example uses the linked manual, revision B-31 (2011), printed pages 5 and 22. Confirm the instructions for the model and version being specified; this example is not a complete room layout.
