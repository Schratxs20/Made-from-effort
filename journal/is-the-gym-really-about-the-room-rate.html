Is the Gym Really About the Room Rate?

By Scott Schratwieser · Published September 28, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026

Every hotel GM and every multifamily developer eventually asks me a version of the same question: does spending more on the gym let us charge more for the room, or the unit?

Fair question. Somebody has to justify the line item, and "it'll just feel nicer" won't survive a budget review.

It's still the wrong question to be asking.

That is why hospitality gym design has to begin with how the space will be used and judged, not a promise of a higher room rate.

01 / What the Rate Question Misses

Picture a guest with five hotel tabs open in the same three-block radius, all roughly the same price. At that point, the properties have mostly converged — comparable lobbies, comparable rooftop bars, comparable pool shots on comparable Instagram grids.

The fitness space is frequently the one area that hasn't converged yet.

For a guest who values training, the gym may help distinguish otherwise similar properties. Whether it influences bookings at a particular hotel is a question to test with guest feedback and booking evidence.

02 / For Multifamily, It's a Different Number Entirely

In an apartment building, I would also ask whether residents use the gym and value keeping access to it. Renewal decisions have many influences. A useful amenity may contribute, but its effect needs evidence from that property.

Ask whether the gym is part of residents' routines before assigning it a retention benefit.

Neither a rent premium nor a retention gain should be assumed from the equipment budget. Track use, feedback, and renewal outcomes, and be clear about what those observations can and cannot establish.

03 / The Question Worth Asking Instead

Keep the financial question, but make the assumptions visible. Occupancy, pricing, location, and other amenities also affect bookings and renewals. Usage and feedback can help evaluate a gym, but do not by themselves prove that it caused a revenue change.

Ask what the gym needs to do for the people entitled to use it. Include access, equipment availability, and maintenance in the brief, then define how the operator will assess those things.

Have the design team establish the applicable accessibility requirements. A hotel and a residents-only apartment amenity should not be assumed to have identical legal scope; other housing and local requirements may apply. U.S. Access Board scope guidance.

The useful starting point is an amenity people can access and use, with a plan to maintain it and evidence to evaluate it.
