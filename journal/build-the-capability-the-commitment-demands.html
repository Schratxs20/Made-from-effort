Build the Capability the Commitment Demands

By Scott Schratwieser · Published November 22, 2026

The usual advice about big goals is to get ready first, then commit once you're actually prepared. Build the capability, then take it out into the world and use it. It sounds responsible. It also explains why so many "someday" goals stay someday goals — because the readiness never quite arrives on its own, and there was never any real pressure forcing it to.

01 / Capability Rarely Shows Up Just Because You're Waiting for It

Sitting around waiting to feel ready is a strategy with a quiet flaw: readiness isn't a thing that accumulates automatically while you wait. It gets built under real pressure, against a real deadline, for a real reason — and without one of those, there's nothing actually forcing the capability to develop. People can prepare "eventually" forever, because eventually never actually arrives with a deadline attached.

A capability you're waiting to have before you commit is usually a capability that only shows up once you've already committed to needing it.

This is the part that gets the order backwards for most people. They treat commitment as the reward for being ready. It's closer to the cause.

02 / Commit to the Outcome First, and Let It Build the Capacity Backward

The move that actually works is to secure the real commitment before the capability exists, then build toward it because you now have no choice but to. Sign up for the event before you're in race shape. Book the session, the competition, the deadline that's real and external — something you can't quietly talk yourself out of the way you can talk yourself out of a vague someday goal. Once that commitment is locked in, the capability isn't optional anymore. It has a deadline and a reason to exist, and that's usually all it was actually missing.

This flips the entire relationship between preparation and commitment. Instead of preparation earning you the right to commit, the commitment becomes the thing that forces the preparation to happen on a schedule, instead of drifting indefinitely.

03 / The Commitment Has to Be Real Enough That Backing Out Costs Something

This only works if the commitment is genuinely external — something with a date, a person, or a consequence attached, not just a private intention that's easy to quietly let slide. A goal you can abandon without anyone noticing or anything changing isn't a forcing function. It's just a preference. The version that actually builds capability is the one where backing out means an actual cost: a missed event you paid for, a person who's expecting you to show up, a public commitment that's awkward to walk back from.

What this means for anyone waiting to feel ready before committing to something real:

  1. Stop treating readiness as a prerequisite for commitment. For most real goals, it's the other way around — the commitment is usually what manufactures the readiness.
  2. Make the commitment external and dated, not private and vague. A deadline nobody else knows about is easy to quietly move. One other people are counting on isn't.
  3. Let the commitment define the capability you need, not the other way around. Figure out what the goal actually requires only after you've already committed to hitting it.
  4. Expect the pressure, and recognize it as the mechanism, not a warning sign. The discomfort of being committed to something you're not fully ready for is exactly what builds the readiness — not a signal you moved too soon.

Waiting to be ready before committing to something real usually just means waiting. Committing first is what actually starts the clock.
