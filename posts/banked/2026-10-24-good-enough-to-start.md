I've noticed a pattern in people who never start.

It's not that they don't care. It's that they're still perfecting the plan.

The right split. The right equipment. The right time of day. The right amount of motivation before the first session counts.

> Waiting for the version you can't improve on is just a slower way of not starting.

Here's what I've come to believe instead: a plan that's good enough, that you actually do, beats a plan that's perfect, that you're still building.

Not because effort doesn't matter. Because momentum does more work than precision ever will.

You learn what you actually need by doing the imperfect version first. You don't learn it by planning the perfect one from the couch.

I see this in training. I see it in gym builds too — the client who waits eight months for the "right" layout instead of building something solid now and adjusting later loses eight months of actual use.

**What this looks like in practice:**

1. **Start with 70% right instead of 100% planned.** The missing 30% reveals itself once you're moving, not before.
2. **Treat the first version as information, not a final answer.** You're allowed to change the plan once you know more than you did on day one.
3. **Notice when "still deciding" is really just "not starting."** Those are two different sentences that feel identical from the inside.

None of this is an argument for sloppy work. It's an argument against the specific trap of mistaking preparation for progress.

> The plan doesn't have to be right. It has to be started.

That's the whole difference between the people who get where they're going and the people still getting ready to leave.
