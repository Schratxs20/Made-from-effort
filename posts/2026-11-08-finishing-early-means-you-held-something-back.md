Ask most people why they stopped a set, ended a session, or called something finished, and the honest answer is usually some version of "it felt done." Not that it hit a real target. Not that the time or effort budget actually ran out. It felt adequate, so it stopped there. That feeling is doing a lot more deciding than most people realize — and it's almost never the same thing as actually finishing.

## 01 / "Good Enough" Is Usually Just "Comfortable"

The first workable version of almost anything arrives well before the real ceiling does. The rep that technically counts. The set that hits the number. The session that checks the box. All of it can look identical to the real thing from the outside while still being a comfort stop rather than an effort stop — a place the mind decided was acceptable, not a place the body or the work actually ran out of road.

> If there was still time or energy left on the clock when you stopped, you didn't finish. You decided.

The uncomfortable part is that most "good enough" stopping points are indistinguishable from real limits unless you're honest about which one you actually hit. Nobody announces "I'm stopping because this got uncomfortable" — it just gets relabeled as done.

## 02 / The Discomfort Near the Limit Is the Signal, Not the Warning

Here's the part that changes how this actually gets used: the discomfort that shows up right before a real limit isn't a reason to back off. It's the marker that you've reached the part that actually counts. The reps, the effort, the material that only exists on the far side of comfortable — that's not accessible by stopping at "good enough," because "good enough" lives entirely on the comfortable side of the line.

This doesn't mean discomfort is always the right signal — a real injury or a genuinely depleted tank is a different thing entirely, and knowing that difference matters. But the garden-variety discomfort that shows up when a set gets hard, or a session gets long, or an idea isn't coming easily — that specific kind of friction is usually exactly where the output that was worth showing up for actually lives.

## 03 / Set the Real Limit First, Then Refuse to Stop Early

None of this works without an actual boundary defined up front — a set number of reps to failure, a session end-time, a hard deadline. Without a real limit, "push past comfortable" has nothing to push against and just turns into aimless grinding. With one, it turns into something specific: hold the standard that nothing gets called finished while there's still real time or capacity left against that limit, and treat the discomfort that shows up near it as confirmation you're close, not as permission to stop.

**What this means for how a session, a set, or anything else that matters gets called finished:**

1. **Define the real limit before you start, not after you feel done.** A rep target, a time cap, a deadline — something external to "how it feels" has to be the actual finish line.
2. **Treat ordinary discomfort near that limit as a signal to keep going, not a reason to stop.** The material on the other side of comfortable is usually the only part that was worth the session.
3. **Know the difference between discomfort and an actual stop condition.** Real pain, real depletion, real risk — those are legitimate reasons to stop. Garden-variety discomfort isn't one of them.
4. **Ask honestly which one you hit.** "It felt done" and "it was actually done" are different claims, and only one of them means you gave the real effort.

Stopping at comfortable isn't a neutral choice. It's choosing not to find out what was on the other side of it.
