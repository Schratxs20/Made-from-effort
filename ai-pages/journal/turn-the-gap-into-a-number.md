# Turn the Gap Into a Number

By [Scott Schratwieser](https://www.madefromeffort.com/about) · Published November 1, 2026

Every so often a client steps up into something genuinely harder than what they're used to — a tougher class, a new group of training partners who are further along, a competition tier they haven't earned a comfortable spot in yet. The instinct in that moment is almost always the same: try harder, push more, want it more. It's well-meaning, and it almost never works, because "try harder" isn't a plan. It's just pressure with nowhere to go.

## 01 / Don't Resolve to Try Harder. Count the Gap.

The useful response to a harder environment isn't more intensity aimed at nothing in particular. It's an honest, specific assessment of exactly where you stand against the people actually in the room with you — including an honest accounting of the advantages they have that you don't. Better background, more experience, a longer head start. Naming that clearly isn't discouraging. It's the only way to know what you're actually solving for.

> Vague resolve dissolves under pressure. A number doesn't.

Most people skip this step because it feels uncomfortable — nobody wants to catalog exactly how far behind they are. But skipping it is exactly why "work harder" so often fails to produce anything specific. You can't close a gap you never measured.

## 02 / Turn the Gap Into One Number, With a Near Date

Once the gap is honestly sized up, the next move is converting it into something concrete: a specific number, attached to a specific, close deadline. Not "get stronger" — a target weight, by a set week. Not "catch up to the group" — a specific number of people in that group you intend to be ahead of, inside a specific window measured in weeks, not someday.

The deadline matters as much as the number. An open-ended goal lets the mind negotiate with itself indefinitely — there's always more time, so there's never real urgency. A near-term deadline forces the issue immediately: either the plan is working this week, or it needs to change this week. That's a dramatically more useful piece of information than a vague sense of progress six months from now.

## 03 / The Real Target Was Never the Other People

Here's the part that keeps this from turning into pure comparison-chasing: the number measured against other people is the tool, not the actual goal. The real target is your own prior ceiling. The harder room, the stronger training partners, the tougher class — all of that is just a forcing function, a reason the old standard stopped being good enough. Once that number gets hit, what actually changed is the client's own baseline, not their ranking against a specific group of people who'll eventually change anyway.

This distinction matters because comparison alone burns out. Chasing a moving target made of other people's progress has no finish line and no real reward. Chasing your own number, even one that was triggered by other people, has both.

**What this means the next time a client — or you — steps into something harder than usual:**

1. **Replace vague resolve with an honest count.** "I need to work harder" isn't a plan. "I'm behind by this specific amount" is the start of one.
2. **Attach a real number and a near deadline to the gap.** Open-ended goals let urgency evaporate. A close deadline doesn't.
3. **Use the external comparison as fuel, not the finish line.** The people ahead of you are the reason to move. Your own improved baseline is what you're actually keeping.
4. **Review the number on schedule, not eventually.** A near-term deadline only works if it's actually checked when it arrives — not quietly pushed back the first time it feels uncomfortable.

Stepping into a harder room isn't a problem that gets solved by wanting it more. It gets solved by doing the uncomfortable math first — and then letting a specific number, not a feeling, decide whether the plan is actually working.
